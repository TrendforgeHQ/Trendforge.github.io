## A New Security Landscape Unfolds
When the U.S. forces that had been stationed in Iraq since 2014 begin their final withdrawal, the country’s newly elected leadership faces a sharp pivot. The government has set a September 30 deadline for all armed groups to hand over weapons to state control, a date chosen to match the end of the U.S.‑led anti‑ISIS coalition. The move signals an attempt to present Iraq as a sovereign state capable of managing its own security while still courting U.S. goodwill.

## Reform or Survival?  The Role of the Coordination Framework
The anti‑corruption campaign and the militia disarmament deadline are framed by experts as a survival strategy for the Coordination Framework rather than substantive reform. The Framework seeks to satisfy Washington’s expectations without confronting Iranian‑aligned power centers. Analysts note that while some parliamentary factions show partial willingness, hard‑line groups designated by the U.S. refuse to comply, indicating that the outcome will be ambiguous rather than decisive.

## What the Deadline Means in Practice
The September 30 target is a cosmetic disarmament process at best. Without clear rules for what happens to surrendered weapons and fighters, compliance is likely to falter. A pathway is needed that explains whether fighters will be integrated into the Iraqi Security Forces, demobilised formally, or receive structured payouts. The absence of such a plan could erode trust and undermine the promise of disarmament.

## Security Risks Beyond Iraq’s Borders
The U.S. withdrawal opens a window for pro‑Iran militias to exploit gaps in Iraq’s defensive posture. Former ambassador Douglas Silliman warned that the Iraqi military lacks the means to counter drones, rockets, and missiles that could be launched from Iraqi territory against Gulf infrastructure. The risk is heightened by past incidents linking Iraqi‑based groups to attacks on regional targets. Monitoring cross‑border threats will be essential to avoid a new cycle of strikes on Iraqi soil.

## Washington’s View and Sanctions Policy
How Washington interprets Baghdad’s actions will shape future sanctions. The U.S. may adjust its stance based on whether Iraq can maintain sovereignty between U.S. and Iranian interests or becomes another flashpoint. The U.S. still offers non‑binding security support, but confidence in Iraq’s ability to defend itself remains mixed.

## The Role of Diplomacy and Monitoring
The first visit of the U.S. envoy Tom Barrack to Iraq in his new role included meetings with Prime Minister Ali al‑Zaidi and Kurdish leaders in Erbil, including KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani. These talks aim to advance trade, energy investment, and federal support. Analysts argue that the real test lies in the day‑to‑day implementation of the disarmament plan and the monitoring of security risks.

## Lessons from the Past and the Path Forward
Iraq’s current dilemma is the outcome of two decades of institutional erosion, sectarian bargaining, and external patronage. Reversing that trajectory will not happen with a single deadline, a single high‑profile visit, or a single change of government. It will require patience, consistency, and long‑term investment—attributes that allowed the problem to take root.

## What Readers Should Watch
Observers should look for concrete, monitorable benchmarks that go beyond rhetoric: verified weapons handover, transparent militia payrolls, and a clear post‑surrender plan for fighters. The government’s ability to navigate the end of the U.S. coalition and to prevent a resurgence of cross‑border attacks will determine whether Iraq can maintain its sovereignty amid regional pressures.
